<QuickAnswer>
- Do the steps **in order**. Each one makes the next one easier.
- Step 1 is free money most people miss: **file your taxes every year, even with little or no income.** Many federal and Alberta benefits only reach you if you file.
- Pay off the most expensive debt first, **one debt at a time**, and call a free non-profit counsellor before you miss payments.
- When you invest, use **low-fee funds** and plan on about **6% a year before fees**, not the 12% you'll hear from some US money personalities.
</QuickAnswer>

## Why a Canadian plan?

Popular US plans, like Dave Ramsey's "Baby Steps", have helped millions of people because they're simple: clear steps, one goal at a time, quick wins. We keep that. But the US versions are built around US accounts (401(k), Roth IRA, 529) and US mortgages, and some of the advice doesn't fit here, or can cost you money.

The biggest differences for Albertans: we have the TFSA and FHSA, government top-ups for kids' education, benefits that depend on filing a tax return, and mortgages that renew every few years.

## Step 1: Claim the money you're owed

**File a tax return every year**, even if you earned little or nothing. The <Official href="https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/child-family-benefits/canada-child-benefit-overview.html" by="CRA">Canada Child Benefit</Official>, the <Official href="https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/child-family-benefits/canada-groceries-essentials-benefit.html" by="CRA">Canada Groceries and Essentials Benefit</Official> (which replaced the GST/HST credit in July 2026), the <Official href="https://www.alberta.ca/alberta-child-and-family-benefit" by="Government of Alberta">Alberta Child and Family Benefit</Official> and the <Official href="https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/child-family-benefits/canada-workers-benefit.html" by="CRA">Canada Workers Benefit</Official> are all worked out from your return.

If you have children, open an RESP. Lower-income families can get the **Canada Learning Bond** without putting in any money of their own.

Volunteers at <Official href="https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/individuals/community-volunteer-income-tax-program.html" by="CRA">free tax clinics</Official> help people with modest incomes file at no cost.

## Step 2: Build a small cushion

Save **$2,000**, or one month of essential bills if that's less. This is for car repairs, a broken fridge, a short gap in pay: the surprises that usually go on a credit card or a payday loan.

Why more than the $1,000 popular US plans use? Their figure is in US dollars, and it hasn't gone up with prices. A Canadian cushion needs to be a little bigger to cover the same surprises.

Keep it in a separate savings account you don't see every day.

## Step 3: Take any free money from your employer

If your employer matches what you put into a pension or group RRSP, **put in enough to get the full match**. A 50% or 100% match is an instant return you can't get anywhere else.

This is where we disagree with Ramsey, who says to pause all investing, matches included, until your consumer debt is paid off and your full emergency fund is saved.

## Step 4: Clear expensive debt, one at a time

1. **Stop new high-cost borrowing first.** In Alberta, a payday loan can cost up to **<Fact id="payday-max-cost-alberta" /> for every $100 you borrow**. On a two-week loan that works out to roughly 365% a year. Pay these off first.
2. Then list your other debts. Pay the minimum on all of them, and put every extra dollar on **one** debt.
3. Pick the smallest balance first if you need a quick win to keep going. But if one debt has a much higher interest rate than the rest, clear that one first: it's costing you the most.
4. When one is gone, roll its payment onto the next.

<Warning label="Before you miss a payment">
Call a free, non-profit credit counsellor **before** things get worse. In Alberta: **Money Mentors** (1-888-294-0076) and the **Credit Counselling Society** (Calgary 403-263-9905, Edmonton 780-701-0083). Be wary of companies that charge upfront to "fix" your debt or your credit.
</Warning>

## Step 5: Build a full emergency fund

Save **three months of essential costs**. Make it **six months or more** if you work in energy, construction, commission or seasonal jobs, or if your household relies on one income. Alberta's economy rises and falls with oil prices, and Employment Insurance replaces only part of your pay.

A **TFSA savings account** is a good home for it: interest is tax-free, and you can take money out any time. The withdrawal room comes back the next year.

## Step 6: Invest 15% for the long term

Aim to put **15% of your gross income**, counting any employer match, towards retirement.

- **Which account?** For most people on modest incomes, the **TFSA** first. In 2026 you can add up to **<Fact id="tfsa-limit-2026" />**. Withdrawals are tax-free and don't reduce federal income-tested benefits. If you're in a higher tax bracket, the **RRSP** often makes more sense. The 2026 limit is 18% of last year's earned income, up to **<Fact id="rrsp-limit-2026" />**.
- **What to buy?** A low-fee, all-in-one index fund (an "asset allocation ETF") holds thousands of companies worldwide in one purchase. Fees matter: a fund charging 2% a year takes ten times what one charging 0.2% does, every year.
- **What to expect?** Canada's financial planning standards body, FP Canada, tells planners to assume returns of about **<Fact id="fpcanada-cdn-equity-return" /> a year for Canadian stocks**, before fees. Plan with numbers like that, not 12%.

## Step 7: Save for your kids and a first home

- **RESP:** the government adds a **Canada Education Savings Grant** of 20% on the first $2,500 you save each year per child, up to <Fact id="cesg-lifetime" /> per child in total.
- **FHSA** (first-time buyers): save up to **<Fact id="fhsa-annual-limit" /> a year** and **<Fact id="fhsa-lifetime-limit" />** in total. You get a tax deduction going in, and it's tax-free coming out for your first home.

## Step 8: Pay off your home early, then build wealth and give

In Canada, mortgage interest on your own home **isn't tax-deductible**, so every extra dollar you pay down saves you the full interest rate. Use your mortgage's yearly **prepayment allowance**. The amount depends on your mortgage contract, so check your agreement. Then keep investing, and give generously.

<HonestTake>
Most money plans fail because they start with guilt and end with a spreadsheet nobody opens. Start with Step 1, because it often finds money you didn't know you had, and do one step at a time. If you slip, you haven't failed. Pick the step back up next payday.
</HonestTake>

## This is general information

These steps are a sensible order for most people, not personal advice. If your situation is complicated, for example a disability, a business, separation or big tax questions, a free non-profit counsellor or a fee-only planner can help.
