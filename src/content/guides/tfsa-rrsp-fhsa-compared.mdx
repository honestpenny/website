<QuickAnswer>
- **Buying your first home?** Start with the **FHSA**: you get a tax deduction going in, and it's tax-free coming out for a first home.
- **Modest income, or might need the money?** The **TFSA** is usually best. Withdrawals are tax-free and don't reduce federal benefits.
- **Higher income now than you expect in retirement?** The **RRSP** usually wins. But it's a tax *delay*, not a tax-free account: you pay tax when you take money out.
- **Kids or a disability?** The **RESP** and **RDSP** come with free government money. Use them first for those goals.
- **Ordinary (non-registered) accounts** come last, once your tax-sheltered room is full.
</QuickAnswer>

## The accounts side by side

| Account | Money going in | Money coming out | Best for | 2026 limit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **TFSA** | No deduction | **Tax-free**, any reason; room comes back next year | Emergency fund, flexible saving, lower incomes, retirees | <Fact id="tfsa-limit-2026" /> a year (up to <Fact id="tfsa-cumulative-room-2026" /> total if eligible since 2009) |
| **RRSP** | **Tax deduction** | **Taxed as income**; room doesn't come back | Retirement, if your tax rate now is higher than it will be later | 18% of last year's earned income, up to <Fact id="rrsp-limit-2026" /> |
| **FHSA** | **Tax deduction** | **Tax-free** for a first home | First-time buyers | <Fact id="fhsa-annual-limit" /> a year, <Fact id="fhsa-lifetime-limit" /> lifetime |
| **RESP** | No deduction; **government adds 20%** | Growth and grants taxed in the student's hands | Children's education after high school | Grant up to <Fact id="cesg-lifetime" /> per child |
| **RDSP** | No deduction; **government grants and bonds** | Partly taxable; early withdrawals can mean paying grants back | People eligible for the Disability Tax Credit | See canada.ca |
| **Non-registered** | No deduction | Tax on interest and dividends each year, and on capital gains when you sell | Once registered room is full | No limit |

## TFSA: the flexible all-rounder

**Pros**
- Growth and withdrawals are **tax-free**.
- Take money out **any time, for anything**. The amount comes back as new room on January 1 of the next year.
- Withdrawals **don't count as income**, so they don't reduce federal income-tested benefits like the Canada Child Benefit, the Canada Groceries and Essentials Benefit or the Guaranteed Income Supplement.

**Cons**
- **No tax deduction** when you put money in.
- **Putting money back in the same year** can push you over your limit. Over-contributions are taxed at **1% a month**.
- It's easy to treat it as a spending account. Keep long-term money invested, not in cash.

## RRSP: powerful, but often misunderstood

**Pros**
- Contributions **reduce your taxable income** now. At $70,000 of income in Alberta, a $5,000 contribution cuts your 2026 tax by about $1,525 (our calculation, 30.5% marginal rate).
- Growth isn't taxed while it stays in the account.
- **Home Buyers' Plan:** borrow up to <Fact id="hbp-limit" /> from your RRSP for a first home, and repay it over 15 years (for first withdrawals from 2022 to 2028, repayments start in the fifth year after you withdraw).
- Employer matching often comes through a group RRSP. Take every dollar of match you're offered.

**Cons, and the tax surprise people don't expect**
- **Every dollar you take out is taxed as income.** Your bank holds back <Fact id="rrsp-withholding-rates" /> depending on the amount, but that's only a down payment on the tax. The withdrawal is added to your income for the year, so depending on your total income you may **owe more at tax time**. In Alberta, the tax on it can be anywhere from 0% (if your total income is below the basic personal amount) up to 48%.
- **The contribution room is gone for good** when you withdraw (except under the Home Buyers' Plan or Lifelong Learning Plan).
- **It can cost you benefits in retirement.** RRSP and RRIF withdrawals count as income, which can reduce the Guaranteed Income Supplement, and for higher incomes trigger the Old Age Security "clawback" (it starts above <Fact id="oas-clawback-threshold" /> of 2025 income, and the threshold rises each year).
- You must close it by the end of the year you turn 71, usually by turning it into a RRIF, which has minimum yearly withdrawals.

**When an RRSP is the wrong choice:** if your income is low now (in Alberta's lowest band, a deduction saves only about 22%), if you expect to receive the Guaranteed Income Supplement in retirement, or if you might need the money in the next few years. In those cases a TFSA usually leaves you better off.

## FHSA: the best deal for first-time buyers

**Pros**
- **Deductible going in, tax-free coming out** for a first home. No other account does both.
- You can **combine it with the Home Buyers' Plan** for the same home, and FHSA money **doesn't have to be repaid**.
- If you don't buy, you can move it to your RRSP **by direct transfer** without using RRSP room. Taking the cash out first makes it taxable.

**Cons**
- Only for **first-time buyers**, with a lifetime cap of <Fact id="fhsa-lifetime-limit" nosource />.
- Unused room only starts building **once you open the account**. Open one early, even with a small amount.

## RESP and RDSP: take the free money

- **RESP:** the government adds a 20% grant on the first $2,500 you save each year per child. Lower-income families can also get the **Canada Learning Bond** without putting in any money.
- **RDSP:** for people eligible for the Disability Tax Credit. Government grants and bonds can add far more than you put in, especially on lower incomes. Money taken out within 10 years of a grant or bond can mean paying some back, so it's best for long-term saving.

## Workplace pensions

- **Defined benefit (DB) pension:** pays a set income in retirement. It's valuable; stay in it if you can.
- **Defined contribution (DC) pension or group RRSP:** you and your employer put money in. **Always contribute enough to get the full employer match.** It's free money.
- Pension contributions reduce your RRSP room (through a "pension adjustment"), shown on your CRA notice of assessment.

## Ordinary (non-registered) accounts

Use these once your TFSA, RRSP and FHSA room is full. You'll pay tax on interest and dividends every year, and on capital gains when you sell. Canadian dividends and capital gains get better tax treatment than interest, so it's usually better to hold your interest-paying savings inside a TFSA or RRSP.

<Warning label="Check your room before you contribute">
Your TFSA and RRSP limits are on <Official href="https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/e-services/cra-login-services.html" by="CRA">CRA My Account</Official>. They don't always include this year's activity, so keep your own record of what you've put in and taken out. Over-contributing costs money every month until you fix it.
</Warning>

<HonestTake>
Most people are told "max out your RRSP" without anyone asking about their income. On a modest Alberta income, a TFSA is usually the smarter first home for your savings: no tax when you take it out, and no hit to your benefits. Use the RRSP when your tax rate is high, the FHSA if you're buying a first home, and always take free government and employer money first.
</HonestTake>
