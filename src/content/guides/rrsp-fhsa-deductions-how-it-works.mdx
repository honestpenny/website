<QuickAnswer>
- **Your RRSP limit is on your notice of assessment** (and in your CRA account). It's 18% of last year's earned income up to <Fact id="rrsp-limit-2026" /> for 2026, minus any workplace pension adjustment, plus unused room from past years.
- **Contributions up to <Fact id="rrsp-deadline-2026-tax-year" /> count for your 2026 return.** The FHSA has no such window: it's the calendar year only.
- **You don't have to deduct right away.** Report every RRSP contribution on Schedule 7, then deduct this year or save it for a higher-income year. FHSA deductions can be saved too.
- **The CRA checks your claims:** your bank, employer and FHSA provider send it slips. You still report and claim them on your return, and keep your own records, especially for the TFSA.
</QuickAnswer>

## Step 1: Find your RRSP limit

Your **RRSP deduction limit** is on the "RRSP deduction limit statement" in your latest **notice of assessment**, and under the savings and pension plans tab in your **CRA account** (My Account). The CRA works it out like this:

1. Unused room carried forward from past years, **plus**
2. 18% of last year's earned income, up to the yearly dollar limit (<Fact id="rrsp-limit-2026" /> for 2026, <Fact id="rrsp-limit-2027" /> for 2027), **minus**
3. Your **pension adjustment** if you're in a workplace pension or DPSP. It's in box 52 of your T4, and it lowers next year's RRSP room because your pension already saves for you. (Some pension changes also create a past service pension adjustment, which lowers room too.)

If you left a pension plan and got less than your pension adjustments assumed, a **pension adjustment reversal** (on a T10 slip) can give some room back.

## Step 2: Contribute, and know the deadline

- Contributions made in the **first 60 days of the year** (up to <Fact id="rrsp-deadline-2026-tax-year" /> for the 2026 tax year) are reported on your **2026** return's Schedule 7. You can deduct them on your 2026 return, or leave them undeducted and claim them in 2027 or a later year.
- Your RRSP provider gives you **contribution receipts** that show when you paid in. Some providers send a separate receipt for the first 60 days of the year. Keep them all.
- **Over-contributing:** you can go up to <Fact id="rrsp-overcontribution-buffer" /> over your limit (if you're 18 or older) without the penalty tax, but you can't deduct it until you have new room. Above that, you pay 1% a month on the amount over the $2,000 buffer, for each month it stays (until you take it out or new room covers it). If you owe the tax, you file form T1-OVP **and pay it** within 90 days after the year ends. Form T3012A lets you take out the excess without tax withheld once the CRA approves it (you report the withdrawal as income and claim an offsetting deduction).

## Step 3: Claim it, or save it for later

On your return, you list **every** RRSP contribution made from March 3, 2026 to March 1, 2027 on **Schedule 7**, even the ones you don't deduct this year. The deduction goes on **line 20800**.

You **don't have to deduct it now**. Contributions you don't deduct carry forward, and you can deduct them in a later year, for example when a raise puts you in a higher bracket. But if you don't report a contribution on Schedule 7 for the tax year it belongs to (a January 1 to March 1, 2027 contribution goes on your **2026** return), the CRA may reduce or disallow your claim later.

The deduction lowers your taxable income, so it saves tax at your **marginal rate**. Whether you get a **refund** depends on whether too much tax came off your pay during the year.

<Warning label="Don't wait for April if you contribute every payday">
If you contribute regularly to your own RRSP, form **T1213** asks the CRA to let your employer take **less tax off each paycheque**, instead of waiting a year for a refund. If you contribute to a group RRSP through payroll, your employer usually reduces the tax automatically.
</Warning>

## How the CRA checks your numbers

| What | Who reports it | Slip or record |
|---|---|---|
| RRSP contributions | Your RRSP provider files them with the CRA (due by May 1 after the deadline) | Contribution receipts |
| Your pension saving | Your employer | T4, box 52 (pension adjustment) |
| Pension room given back | Your pension plan | T10 (pension adjustment reversal) |
| RRSP withdrawals | Your RRSP provider | T4RSP |
| RRIF payments | Your RRIF provider | T4RIF |
| FHSA contributions, transfers and withdrawals | Your FHSA provider | T4FHSA |

The CRA uses these slips to check your return and shows the result on your notice of assessment. If something doesn't match, it may ask you for receipts or reassess your return.

<Warning label="TFSA room in your CRA account can be out of date">
The CRA says to use **your own records** for TFSA room, not the figure in your CRA account. Banks only report a year's TFSA transactions at the end of February of the next year, so the CRA account can miss recent deposits and withdrawals. Over-contributing costs 1% a month.
</Warning>

## Spousal RRSPs: the three-year rule

If you pay into your spouse's or partner's RRSP, **you** get the deduction. But if they take money out in a year you contributed, or in either of the two years before, the withdrawal is usually taxed in **your** hands, up to what you contributed in those three years (form T2205 works out the amount).

## The FHSA, step by step

1. **Check you qualify.** You must be a **resident of Canada** (for tax purposes), be at least 18 (19 in some provinces) and 71 or younger on December 31 of the year you open it, and be a **first-time home buyer**: you didn't live in a home you or your spouse or partner owned, as your main home, this year or in the previous four calendar years.
2. **Open it early.** Your FHSA room **only starts when you open your first account** (opening also starts its 15-year clock). Opening one this year, even with nothing in it, gives you this year's room to carry forward.
3. **Contribute up to <Fact id="fhsa-annual-limit" /> a year**, <Fact id="fhsa-lifetime-limit" /> in total. Unused room carries forward, but only up to $8,000, so the most you can add in one year is $16,000. Contributions count for the **calendar year only**: there's no first-60-days rule like the RRSP.
4. **Deduct it now or later.** FHSA contributions go on **Schedule 15** and are deducted on **line 20805**. Like the RRSP, you can save the deduction for a later year, even after the account is closed. File Schedule 15 for the year you open the account, even if you put nothing in.
5. **Withdraw tax-free for your first home** using form **RC725**. When you withdraw, you need a written agreement to buy or build a home that closes (or is finished) before October 1 of the year after the withdrawal, and you must intend to live there within one year after buying or building it. If any condition isn't met, the withdrawal is taxed. You can also use the RRSP **Home Buyers' Plan** for the same home.
6. **Didn't buy?** The FHSA must close by December 31 of the year of the earliest of: the 15th anniversary of opening it, the year you turn 71, or the year after your first home withdrawal. You can move it **directly** to your RRSP or RRIF with no immediate tax and without using RRSP room. It's taxed later, when you take it out of the RRSP or RRIF. Don't withdraw it yourself first: that's taxed.

Moving money from an RRSP into an FHSA (a direct transfer, form RC720) uses FHSA room, isn't deductible again, and doesn't give you back the RRSP room.

<HonestTake>
Most of the stress around RRSPs and FHSAs comes from not knowing where the numbers live. They're on your notice of assessment and in your CRA account, and your bank and employer send the CRA slips to check against. Do three things: open an FHSA early if there's any chance you'll buy a first home (it starts the room and the 15-year clock), report every RRSP contribution even if you deduct it later, and keep your own running total for your TFSA.
</HonestTake>
